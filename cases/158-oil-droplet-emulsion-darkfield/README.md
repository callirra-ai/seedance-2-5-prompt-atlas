## Prompt

```text
The droplets you photographed hang suspended in clear liquid and must stay exactly where they are while they slowly merge. One large drop drifts into a smaller one and the two become a single sphere with a clean bright outline, wobbling once before it settles. The camera pushes past the near droplet and lets it fall out of focus into the emulsion behind. Dark-field lighting keeps the background black and rings each droplet with light, no fill anywhere. Slow, silent, scientific, evening studio.
```

## Why it works

Merging droplets are a physics claim, so the prompt states the rule plainly: the drops stay where they are, one drifts in, a single sphere forms, one wobble, settle. That sequence gives the model a beginning and an end instead of leaving it to improvise motion. Eight seconds covers the merge and the settle without padding. Dark-field lighting is named as a technique because it produces a specific look — black field, bright droplet outlines — that flat studio light cannot imitate, and the push past the near drop adds depth without extra elements.

## Reference image

A macro or microscope-style photo of droplets suspended in clear liquid against a plain background. Avoid photos with visible bubbles, labels or particles, since the model will animate every bright spot it can see and stray highlights become drifting objects.
